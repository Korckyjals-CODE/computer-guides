Risk Management: Social Worksheet

Grade 11 · Worksheet · About 25 minutes
Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

Name: Grade/Section: Date:
How to use this worksheet
  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.
  • Each part has a time so you know how long to spend.
  • Read every instruction before you write. You can do this on your own.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Risk decisions are never only technical. Communities decide whose harms count, how openly to discuss threats, and when “accept the risk” is fair. In this worksheet you will examine values, equity, communication, and ethics in digital risk management.

Do this:

  1. Name a risk decision that affected a group (school policy, app privacy setting, event livestream).
  2. Who seemed to benefit most from that decision—and who bore more downside?

Part 2 – Whose risk counts? (about 5 minutes)

Impact looks different depending on position. A “low” impact for administrators may be high for students who lose access, privacy, or opportunity. Good risk management asks who is harmed, not only average organizational loss.

Real-life scenario: Leadership accepts the risk of skipping MFA on the career-services portal “to reduce login friction.” Staff save a few seconds. Students’ internship applications and recommendation letters sit behind weaker accounts.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: How did “accept the risk” shift harm onto people with less power to refuse?


Do this: Label each framing Org-centered or People-centered (best fit).

Framing of impactType
“Outage costs IT overtime hours.”
“Seniors miss scholarship deadlines if the portal fails.”
“A leaked roster could expose students in vulnerable situations.”

Part 3 – Transparency, fear, and rumor (about 5 minutes)

Talking about risks can create anxiety—or build trust. Hiding known gaps often worsens harm when something fails. Ethical communication balances honesty with calm, actionable information: what is known, what is being done, and what people should do now.

Real-life scenario: An audit finds unencrypted student health forms on a shared drive. Leadership waits “until after open house” to avoid bad press. A screenshot leaks on social media with exaggerated claims. Trust collapses faster than a controlled disclosure would have.

Critical thinking: Draft 2–3 sentences of an honest, non-panic update to families when a serious data-handling gap is found.



Part 4 – Ethics of accept, avoid, and mitigate (about 5 minutes)

Avoiding a risk (canceling a useful service) can protect privacy but reduce opportunity. Mitigating costs time and money. Accepting residual risk can be responsible—or a quiet way to ignore people who cannot opt out. Ethics asks whether those who accept the risk are the same people who face the impact.

Real-life scenario: A research class wants to scrape public social posts for a project. Avoid: ban the assignment. Mitigate: use only consented, anonymized samples with teacher review. Accept without controls: speed for the grade, high privacy harm for strangers.

Do this:

  1. Pick Avoid, Mitigate, or Accept for the research scenario and justify in terms of who is protected.

  2. Name one school digital risk where accepting without consulting affected students would be unfair.

Part 5 – Stakeholder risk board (about 5 minutes)

Do this:

  1. Sketch a four-box board for a risk decision: Technical facts · Who is affected · Communication plan · Ethical treatment choice (mitigate / avoid / accept).
  1. Explain why skipping “Who is affected” makes the other boxes incomplete.

Part 6 – Check what you learned (about 2 minutes)

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.

  • □ I can question whose harms count in a risk decision.
  • □ I can contrast org-centered and people-centered impact framing.
  • □ I can argue for honest, calm risk communication.
  • □ I can evaluate ethical tensions among avoid, mitigate, and accept.
  • □ I can outline a stakeholder-aware risk decision board.

When all boxes are checked, you have reviewed the main ideas from this worksheet.
