Risk Management: Technical Worksheet

Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

How to use this worksheet
  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.
  • Each part has a time so you know how long to spend.
  • Read every instruction before you write. You can do this on your own.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Risk management is a structured cycle: identify what could go wrong, assess how likely and how severe it is, then choose to mitigate, transfer, avoid, or accept the risk. You will practice separating likelihood from impact and documenting reasoned decisions for digital systems.

Do this:

  1. Name one digital risk for a school or personal project (data loss, account takeover, outage before a deadline).
  2. In a few words, is your concern mainly about how often it might happen, how bad it would be, or both?

Part 2 – Identify risks vs vague fears (about 5 minutes)

A useful risk statement names an asset (what you care about), a threat or hazard, and a possible consequence. “Something bad with the network” is too vague to manage. Clear identification enables assessment and controls.

Real-life scenario: A robotics club stores competition code only on one laptop. Vague fear: “We might lose stuff.” Clear risk: “If the laptop disk fails before finals, we lose the only build and cannot compete.”

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Why does rewriting vague fears into asset–threat–consequence statements help teams act?


Do this: Rewrite each vague statement as a clearer risk (asset + what could happen + consequence).

Vague statementClearer risk
“The cloud is risky.”
“Phones are a problem.”
“Wi-Fi might mess up.”

Part 3 – Likelihood vs impact (about 5 minutes)

Likelihood is how probable an event is. Impact is how severe the harm would be if it occurs. High impact + low likelihood (rare catastrophic disk failure) and high likelihood + lower impact (frequent brief Wi-Fi blips) need different responses. Rough scales (Low / Medium / High) are enough for classroom analysis.

Real-life scenario: Risk A: ransomware encrypts the student information system (low–medium likelihood for a well-patched school, very high impact). Risk B: a club printer jams weekly (high likelihood, low impact if a backup printer exists).

Critical thinking: Which risk deserves priority spending if the budget allows only one major control—and why? Use likelihood and impact in your answer.


Do this: Rate each risk L / M / H for likelihood and impact (your reasoned judgment).

RiskLikelihoodImpact
Forgotten USB with unencrypted club passwords is lost on a bus
Brief Wi-Fi drop during advisory announcements
No tested backup of graduation photo archives before a disk failure

Part 4 – Treat risks: mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept (about 5 minutes)

After assessment, choose a treatment: Mitigate (reduce likelihood or impact with controls), Transfer (shift some consequence, e.g. insurance or a vendor SLA), Avoid (do not do the activity), or Accept (knowingly live with residual risk when cost of more control outweighs benefit). Acceptance should be deliberate—not accidental neglect.

Real-life scenario: A journalism site collects tips. Mitigate: MFA and encryption. Avoid: refuse anonymous uploads entirely (hurts mission). Accept: residual risk that a determined attacker still breaches despite controls—documented and reviewed yearly.

Do this: Choose the best-fit treatment for each case (Mitigate / Transfer / Avoid / Accept).

SituationTreatment
Store competition code on one laptop; add cloud + offline backups and weekly restore tests
Cancel a public live stream of unfinished exam questions after spotting a leak risk
Buy device insurance for loaner laptops taken on field trips
Keep using a popular free plugin with a known low-severity bug; document why and revisit next term

Write one residual risk you would still accept after mitigating the robotics-laptop scenario (what remains, and why acceptance is reasonable).

Part 5 – Risk register sketch (about 5 minutes)

Do this:

  1. Sketch a mini risk register with four columns for one school digital service: Risk · Likelihood · Impact · Treatment. Fill at least two rows.
  1. Star the risk you would treat first and justify using likelihood × impact thinking.

Part 6 – Check what you learned (about 2 minutes)

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.

  • □ I can write a clear risk (asset, threat, consequence)—not only a vague fear.
  • □ I can separate likelihood from impact.
  • □ I can choose among mitigate / transfer / avoid / accept.
  • □ I can explain deliberate acceptance of residual risk.
  • □ I can sketch a simple risk register and prioritize a treatment.

When all boxes are checked, you have reviewed the main ideas from this worksheet.
