## Part 2 – Whose risk counts? (about 5 minutes)

Impact looks different depending on position. A “low” impact for administrators may be high for students who lose access, privacy, or opportunity. Good risk management asks **who is harmed**, not only average organizational loss.

**Real-life scenario:** Leadership accepts the risk of skipping MFA on the career-services portal “to reduce login friction.” Staff save a few seconds. Students’ internship applications and recommendation letters sit behind weaker accounts.

**Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing:** How did “accept the risk” shift harm onto people with less power to refuse?

## Part 3 – Transparency, fear, and rumor (about 5 minutes)

Talking about risks can create anxiety—or build trust. Hiding known gaps often worsens harm when something fails. Ethical communication balances honesty with calm, actionable information: what is known, what is being done, and what people should do now.

**Real-life scenario:** An audit finds unencrypted student health forms on a shared drive. Leadership waits “until after open house” to avoid bad press. A screenshot leaks on social media with exaggerated claims. Trust collapses faster than a controlled disclosure would have.

**Critical thinking:** Draft 2–3 sentences of an honest, non-panic update to families when a serious data-handling gap is found.

## Part 4 – Ethics of accept, avoid, and mitigate (about 5 minutes)

**Avoiding** a risk (canceling a useful service) can protect privacy but reduce opportunity. **Mitigating** costs time and money. **Accepting** residual risk can be responsible—or a quiet way to ignore people who cannot opt out. Ethics asks whether those who accept the risk are the same people who face the impact.

**Real-life scenario:** A research class wants to scrape public social posts for a project. Avoid: ban the assignment. Mitigate: use only consented, anonymized samples with teacher review. Accept without controls: speed for the grade, high privacy harm for strangers.
