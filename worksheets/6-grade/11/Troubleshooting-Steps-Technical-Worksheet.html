Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Troubleshooting means using clear steps to find and fix a technology problem. You notice the symptom (what you see), gather clues, try safe checks, and know when to restart or ask for help. In this worksheet you will practice an ordered troubleshooting process.

Do this:

  1. Name one tech problem you have seen (app freeze, no sound, blank screen, no Wi-Fi).
  2. Write the first thing you or someone else tried.

Part 2 – A simple step sequence (about 5 minutes)

A useful order is: Observe → Describe → Check basics → Try one change → Test → Ask for help if needed. Jumping around or changing many things at once makes it hard to know what worked.

Real-life scenario: Maya’s tablet will not play video sound. She turns the volume up, then toggles mute, then plugs in headphones—one check at a time. She discovers mute was on. Her friend Sam taps random settings and still does not know why sound returned.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Why does trying one change at a time help more than tapping many settings at once?

Part 3 – Symptoms and basic checks (about 5 minutes)

A symptom is what you observe (“no picture,” “spinning wheel,” “error message”). Basic checks include power, cables, volume, storage warnings, and whether the problem is on one device or many. Write error words down—they are clues.

Real-life scenario: A classroom projector shows “No Signal.” The laptop is on. The HDMI cable looks loose at the projector end. Another student says, “The projector is broken,” without checking the cable.

Critical thinking: What symptom should you write first, and which basic check comes next?


Do this: Match each symptom with a good first check from the word bank.

Part 4 – Restart, document, escalate (about 5 minutes)

Restarting can clear temporary glitches, but it is not the only step—and it is not safe when you smell smoke or see sparks. Document what you tried. Escalate means asking a trusted adult or IT when the problem is risky, repeats, or is beyond what you can fix safely.

Real-life scenario: An app freezes once with no smell or heat. Restarting fixes it. Later, the same error code appears every morning. Another day, a charger tip sparks when plugged in.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: When is restart enough, and when should you stop and get help?

Do this: Choose the best action: Restart, Document & report, or Stop & get help.

SituationBest action

Part 5 – Draw a troubleshooting flow (about 5 minutes)

Do this:

  1. Sketch a flow with boxes: Observe → Describe → Check basics → One change → Test → Fix or Ask for help.
  1. Circle the step you most often skip, and write why it matters.
