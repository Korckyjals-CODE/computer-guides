Troubleshooting Steps: Social Worksheet

Grade 6 · Worksheet · About 25 minutes
Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

How to use this worksheet
  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.
  • Each part has a time so you know how long to spend.
  • Read every instruction before you write. You can do this on your own.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Troubleshooting is also about people. Calm words, clear reports, and teamwork help groups solve problems without blame. In this worksheet you will practice helpful habits when tech fails in class or at home.

Do this:

  1. Name a shared place where tech problems affect more than one person (lab, cart, home computer).
  2. Write one feeling people often have when something breaks mid-task.

Part 2 – Calm reporting, not blame (about 5 minutes)

A good report names the symptom and what you already tried. Blaming (“You broke it!”) makes people hide problems. Calm facts help the next helper faster.

Real-life scenario: The class printer stops. Kai yells, “Who jammed it again?” Everyone goes quiet. Later they learn a paper tray was empty—not a jam, and no one “broke” it.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: How does blame slow troubleshooting? What could Kai say instead?


Do this: Mark each line Helpful report or Blame / not helpful.

What someone saysHelpful or Blame?
“Laptop #4 shows a black screen; power light is on; I checked the cable.”
“You always mess up the computers.”
“The Wi-Fi icon has an X; other devices nearby work.”

Part 3 – Helping without taking over (about 5 minutes)

Peers can help by asking questions, suggesting one safe check, or writing clues together. Grabbing the device and fixing everything alone can leave classmates confused next time.

Real-life scenario: Riley’s slideshow will not open. A friend snatches the laptop, clicks for two minutes, fixes it, and walks away without explaining. Riley still does not know what to try next time.

Critical thinking: Write one helpful question a peer could ask Riley instead of taking over.


Do this: Circle the wiser peer choice.

SituationWiser choice (circle)

Part 4 – Fair turn-taking when time is short (about 5 minutes)

When a device fails near a deadline, stress rises. Fair habits include sharing backups of work, rotating who uses the working machine, and agreeing who will report the issue.

Real-life scenario: Two partners share one lab computer. It freezes 10 minutes before the end of class. One partner refuses to let the other save a copy to a USB while they “keep trying.” Both lose progress.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: What fair step should come first when time is almost up?

Do this: Write two fair-team rules for troubleshooting under time pressure.

Part 5 – Helpful helper poster (about 5 minutes)

Do this:

  1. Design a poster with three social troubleshooting habits (stay calm, report facts, ask before taking over, save work first).
  1. Explain who benefits when the class follows the poster.

Part 6 – Check what you learned (about 2 minutes)

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.

  • □ I can give a calm, factual problem report instead of blaming.
  • □ I can help a peer without taking over the whole fix.
  • □ I know why writing error messages helps the group.
  • □ I can suggest fair rules when time is short and tech fails.
  • □ I can design a short helper poster for my class.

When all boxes are checked, you have reviewed the main ideas from this worksheet.
