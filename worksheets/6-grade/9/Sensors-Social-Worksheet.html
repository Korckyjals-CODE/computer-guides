Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Sensors help systems react, and they also decide who gets noticed. In this worksheet you will think about comfort and fairness, the difference between sensing motion and recording people, who should see sensor data, and what a school should ask before it installs sensors.

Do this:

  1. Circle the question you most want to think about: Who gets left out · What gets recorded · Who sees the data · Where sensors belong
  2. Write one sentence about a sensor you have noticed at school, in a store, or on a street.

Part 2 – Who the sensor treats as “there” (about 5 minutes)

A sensor follows its settings. It does not know why a person is still, small, slow, or outside its view. A system that only trusts that sensor can be unfair even when nobody meant to leave someone out.

Real-life scenario: Hallway lights use a motion sensor. They turn off after a short time with no movement. You stop to read a poster. A classmate who uses a wheelchair rolls through more slowly than the timer expects. In both cases the hallway goes dark.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Who is the system failing? What change to the timer, the sensor height, or the rule would include more people?

Do this: For each person, write what the motion sensor might do and one fairer setting.

Person What a short-timer motion sensor might do A fairer setting or backup
A student standing still to read

Part 3 – Noticing is not the same as recording (about 5 minutes)

A motion sensor can turn on a light and then forget the moment. A camera can record faces, save video, and let someone watch later. Both are sensors. The effect on people is different. Consent means people know what is collected and have a real chance to agree, especially in a place they must use, like a school.

Real-life scenario: A school wants a sensor in the cafeteria “for safety.” One plan uses a motion sensor that only counts how many people are in the room. Another plan uses cameras that store video for 30 days.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Which plan answers a safety question with less recording? What should students and families be told before either plan starts?

Do this: Compare the two plans.

Part 4 – Who should see the data (about 5 minutes)

Sensor data can help a building run. It can also follow people more closely than they expect. A store sensor might count customers so the shop knows when to open another register. The same kind of sensor might also estimate how long you stood at one shelf.

Critical thinking: Counting visitors can be useful. Tracking how long one person stands in one spot is a different choice. Write one use you would allow and one use you would not allow. Give a reason for each.

Part 5 – Where a sensor belongs (about 5 minutes)

A school should be able to say what a sensor is for, what it stores, and where it does not belong. Bathrooms, changing rooms, and private conversations are different from a dark hallway that only needs a light.

Do this:

  1. Sketch a simple school map (hallway, classroom, cafeteria, bathroom door). Mark one place you would put a sensor and one place you would not. Label the purpose of the sensor you allow.
