Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: The social impact of an automated service is not the average on a dashboard. In this worksheet you will ask who gains, who is shut out, how work moves, and what a public agency should answer before it removes the person who used to help.

Do this:

  1. Name a public place where a machine or an app replaced a staffed desk (a station, a library, a clinic check-in, a school entrance—any example).
  2. Write one sentence about a person who uses that place but did not choose the new system.

Part 2 – The average is not the impact (about 5 minutes)

An agency can hit its goal and still shift the cost onto people who have fewer options. A delay statistic describes the system. It does not describe the rider who cannot enter.

Real-life scenario: A transit agency closes the staffed booth at a station. Gates open with an app or a transit card. The agency reports fewer delays at the gate. Riders without a working app, or whose card fails, stand outside the gate. No one on the platform is assigned to help them.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Who is the “fewer delays” number for? Who does that number leave out? What would you measure besides delay?

Part 3 – The job moved (about 5 minutes)

Automation rarely deletes work. It moves it. The booth worker stops selling tickets and answering the same question. Someone else, often unnamed, cleans sensors, resets gates, handles appeals, and explains fines. If the new tasks are not in a job description, the system looks self-running and the person holding it up stays invisible.

Real-life scenario: After the booth closes, gate errors rise on Monday mornings. A supervisor resets them between other duties. Riders who were charged twice file forms that no posted role is responsible for reading.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Which task disappeared from the platform, and which task appeared somewhere else? Why does it matter that no role owns the new task?

Do this: Write No longer done by a person on the platform, Still a person’s job, or Appeared after the change.

Task Classification
Selling a ticket and answering a face-to-face question
Deciding an appeal when a rider is double-charged

Explaining to a family why the gate will not open

Part 4 – Facts before the booth closes (about 5 minutes)

A decision about a place people must use should survive questions that have factual answers: what is stored, how long, who can still get help, and whether trip data can be sold. “Smoother travel” is not an answer to any of those.

Critical thinking: The vendor may sell “anonymized” path data from the gates. Removing names does not finish the question. Write what a buyer could still learn about a neighborhood, and one contract limit you would require.

Do this: Write Agency, Vendor, Riders, or More than one for who should control the decision.

Part 6 – Check what you learned (about 2 minutes)

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.

  • □ I can show that a better average can hide who is shut out of a service.
  • □ I can name benefits and barriers for the agency, regular users, and people without the required tool.
  • □ I can explain how automation moves work instead of erasing it.
  • □ I can limit who may keep or sell data from a system people must use.
  • □ I can write a recommendation that keeps a benefit, sets a limit, and leaves a person available when the system fails.

When all boxes are checked, you have reviewed the main ideas from this worksheet.
