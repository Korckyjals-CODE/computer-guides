Failure Analysis: Social Worksheet

Grade 10 · Worksheet · About 25 minutes
Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

Name: Grade/Section: Date:
How to use this worksheet
  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.
  • Each part has a time so you know how long to spend.
  • Read every instruction before you write. You can do this on your own.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: When systems fail, people feel stress, blame, and pressure to “just make it work.” Strong teams practice blameless learning, clear communication, and honesty about impact. In this worksheet you will analyze the human side of failure analysis.

Do this:

  1. Name a failure that affected more than one person.
  2. Write one emotion people might feel during that failure.

Part 2 – Blame culture vs learning culture (about 5 minutes)

A blame culture hunts for a person to punish. A learning culture asks what in the system (tools, training, time, process) allowed the failure—and how to improve. Blame often hides information; learning surfaces it.

Real-life scenario: A shared drive folder is deleted. Leadership demands “Who did it?” before asking whether recycle bin/versioning was enabled, who had delete rights, or whether a checklist existed for archive moves.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: How might fear of blame stop someone from reporting an early mistake?


Do this: Label each response Blame-first or Learning-first.

Response after a failureType
“You're careless—don't touch the server again.”
“Let's list what permissions and backups were missing.”
“Write the steps that failed and one safeguard we will add.”

Part 3 – Communicating during downtime (about 5 minutes)

Clear status updates reduce rumor. Good messages state what is down, who is affected, what is known, and when the next update will arrive—even if the cause is still unknown.

Real-life scenario: The grading portal fails before a deadline. Silence for three hours. Students panic-email teachers; rumors say “grades were wiped.” Actually only login is down; data is intact.

Critical thinking: Draft a 2–3 sentence status update that is honest without blaming anyone.



Part 4 – Impact, equity, and ethical reporting (about 5 minutes)

Failures hit people unevenly: students with less home internet, one shared family device, or tight deadlines suffer more. Ethical reporting means sharing accurate facts, not exaggerating for drama or hiding to protect image.

Real-life scenario: Wi-Fi fails during a timed online quiz. Some students finish on mobile data; others cannot. A fair response may extend time or offer an alternate format—not insist “everyone had the same chance.”

Do this:

  1. List two groups who might be hit harder by a school system outage.
  2. Write one fairness action decision-makers could take after analyzing the failure.

Part 5 – Team post-failure sketch (about 5 minutes)

Do this:

  1. Sketch a simple “after-action” board with four boxes: Facts · Impact on people · What we change · How we communicate.
  1. Explain why “Impact on people” belongs next to technical facts.

Part 6 – Check what you learned (about 2 minutes)

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.

  • □ I can contrast blame culture with learning culture.
  • □ I can draft a clear downtime status message.
  • □ I can explain how failures affect people unequally.
  • □ I value ethical, accurate reporting over rumor or cover-ups.
  • □ I can outline a people-aware after-action review.

When all boxes are checked, you have reviewed the main ideas from this worksheet.
