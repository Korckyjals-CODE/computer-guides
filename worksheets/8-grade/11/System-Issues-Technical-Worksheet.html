System Issues: Technical Worksheet

Grade 8 · Worksheet · About 25 minutes
Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

Name: Grade/Section: Date:
How to use this worksheet
  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.
  • Each part has a time so you know how long to spend.
  • Read every instruction before you write. You can do this on your own.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: A system issue is when hardware, software, network, or configuration stops a service from working as expected. You will classify issue types, describe symptoms with evidence, run basic isolation questions, and decide when to restart versus document and escalate.

Do this:

  1. Describe one system issue you have seen (black screen, no Wi-Fi, app crash, login failure).
  2. Write the first symptom people noticed.

Part 2 – Types of system issues (about 5 minutes)

Classifying helps you choose the first tests: hardware (physical parts), software (apps/OS), network (connectivity and online services), or configuration (settings, accounts, permissions). Wrong categories waste time.

Real-life scenario: Nobody in the lab can open the online classroom, but local files and offline videos still work. Monitors and projectors are fine.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Why does this case point more to network or an online service than to a broken screen?


Do this: Label each case Hardware, Software, Network, or Configuration.

SituationType
Ethernet cable is cut behind the desk
An app crashes whenever one specific file opens
The account has no permission to print
Fan fails and the device shuts down from heat

Part 3 – Symptoms, isolation, and evidence (about 5 minutes)

Strong diagnosis records: what fails, since when, how many devices or accounts, and what changed recently. Change one variable at a time. Capture error text—guessing without evidence slows everyone down.

Real-life scenario: Only computer 7 cannot print. Computers 6 and 8 work. Yesterday someone moved desk 7 and the cable looked loose afterward.

Critical thinking: What first test would you run, and what evidence would you look for?


Do this: Write three isolation questions for the report “Wi-Fi keeps dropping.”

Part 4 – Restart, document, and escalate (about 5 minutes)

Restarting may clear temporary states, but it is not analysis if the issue returns. Documenting (symptom, tests, result) helps support staff. Escalating means requesting help when risk or complexity is beyond safe self-repair.

Real-life scenario: A student restarts three times a machine that smells like burning and keeps forcing power on. The odor signals a possible electrical fault that needs an adult or IT—not more retries.

Do this: Mark Restart first, Document & report, or Stop & get help.

SituationBest action
An app froze once; no smell or heat
The same error returns each morning with a visible code
Sparks at the outlet

Write a short incident note (symptom / test done / next step).



Part 5 – Diagnosis map (about 5 minutes)

Do this:

  1. Draw a flow: Observe symptom → Classify type → Isolation questions → Safe test → Resolved? → Document or escalate.
  1. Mark the step people most often skip in your setting, and explain why it matters.

Part 6 – Check what you learned (about 2 minutes)

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.

  • □ I can explain what a system issue is and give an example.
  • □ I can classify issues as hardware, software, network, or configuration.
  • □ I can ask isolation questions and use symptoms with evidence.
  • □ I know when to restart, when to document, and when to ask for help.
  • □ I can sketch a basic diagnosis flow.

When all boxes are checked, you have reviewed the main ideas from this worksheet.
