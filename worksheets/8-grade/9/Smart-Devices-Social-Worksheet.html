Smart Devices: Social Worksheet

Grade 8 · Worksheet · About 25 minutes
Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

Name: Grade/Section: Date:
How to use this worksheet
  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.
  • Each part has a time so you know how long to spend.
  • Read every instruction before you write. You can do this on your own.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: A smart device in a shared place affects people who did not choose every setting. In this worksheet you will think about consent at home, who holds control, what happens when a “free” device depends on an account, and a house rule that keeps the useful part without ignoring guests and family.

Do this:

  1. Name a smart device that more than one person might share.
  2. Circle the issue you want to examine: Being overheard · Who holds the controls · An account you cannot leave · People left out of the setup

Part 2 – A device that shares the room (about 5 minutes)

A smart speaker can set timers and play music. Many also listen for a wake word, then send that request to a service. People who live there may know this. A guest, a younger sibling, or a neighbor at the table may not. Consent here means a person knows a device may capture their voice and can ask that it be off.

Real-life scenario: A family uses a smart speaker in the kitchen. During dinner, a guest tells a private story about a job search. The speaker’s light flashes, as it does when it thinks it heard the wake word. Nobody warned the guest.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: What should the family have said before dinner? What should they do in the moment the light flashes?

Do this: Rate each practice. Write Respectful, Unclear, or Not okay.

Practice Your rating One reason
Telling guests the speaker is on and how to mute it
Leaving a speaker on in a bedroom used by visitors
Reviewing saved voice requests and deleting ones the family did not mean to store
Using the speaker to listen to another room without telling the people there

Part 3 – Who holds the controls (about 5 minutes)

In many homes one phone owns the accounts. That person can change the lock, the thermostat, and the lights. Other people depend on them. Convenience for the account owner can become a single point of control for everyone else.

Real-life scenario: One parent’s phone controls the smart lock. A teenager gets home while the parent is in a meeting and the parent’s phone battery is dead. The keypad code was never shared because “the app is easier.”

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Who has control, who is locked out, and what backup would still be fair if the family wants the app?

Do this: Propose who should be able to do each action. You may name more than one person.

Action Who should be able to do it? Why
Unlock the door from the street
See a history of every time the door opened
Change the temperature while people are home
Add or remove someone from the account

Part 4 – When “free” still has a cost (about 5 minutes)

Some smart devices are cheap because they need an account, an app, and a company service. If the company shuts the service down, changes the rules, or requires a new subscription, the device may become a normal object that no longer does the job people bought it for. People who cannot make an account, or who do not want one, are left out of a product that looks ordinary on the shelf.

Critical thinking: A smart bulb is inexpensive and needs a free account. Two years later the app starts charging a monthly fee to turn the lights on from a phone. The wall switch still works. Is the family stuck? Write what they keep, what they lose, and whether the original price told the truth about the cost.

Do this: Compare two choices for a family buying a lamp.

Lamp with a normal switch Cheap smart bulb that needs an account
Works during a Wi-Fi outage
Works if the company closes the app
Can be shared without giving someone an account
Which would you recommend, and why?

Part 5 – A house rule (about 5 minutes)

A short rule can protect people without banning every useful feature. A good rule says what the device is for, when it should be muted or off, and who can see its history.

  2. Sketch the room where the device sits. Mark where it may stay on and where it must be muted or off. Label who is usually in each spot.
  1. Critical thinking: Name one person your rule might still leave out (a guest, a younger child, a relative who does not use the app). What extra step would include them?

Part 6 – Check what you learned (about 2 minutes)

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.

  • □ I can explain why people who share a room deserve to know a smart device may capture sound or activity.
  • □ I can describe a single point of control and a fairer backup.
  • □ I can separate a device’s useful job from the account or fee it depends on.
  • □ I can compare a normal product with a connected one on outages, shutdowns, and sharing.
  • □ I can write a house rule, sketch where a device may stay on, and notice who that rule might still forget.

When all boxes are checked, you have reviewed the main ideas from this worksheet.
