Human Input Limits: Social Worksheet

Grade 8 · Worksheet · About 25 minutes
Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

Name: Grade/Section: Date:
How to use this worksheet
  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.
  • Each part has a time so you know how long to spend.
  • Read every instruction before you write. You can do this on your own.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Recognizing human limits shapes fair policies—timed tests, accessibility, and how we treat mistakes made under stress.

Do this:

  1. Name a school rule or system that assumes people never get tired or distracted.
  2. Circle a fairer stance: Design for real humans · Punish every slip equally without context

Part 2 – Empathy over mockery (about 5 minutes)

Laughing at typos or slow clicks ignores limits of stress, language, and ability. Empathy improves collaboration and inclusion.

Real-life scenario: A student learning in a second language is mocked for slow form completion during a group task.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: What supportive response replaces mockery?

Part 3 – Equity in timed input (about 5 minutes)

Strict timers on typing-heavy tasks can unfairly punish students with motor, language, or attention differences—even when they know the content.

Real-life scenario: A “speed typing warm-up” counts for a grade. A student with hand strain scores low despite strong understanding.

Critical thinking: How could assessment separate understanding from raw input speed?

Do this:

  1. Propose one alternative assessment format.
  2. Circle equitable practice: Multiple ways to respond · Only the fastest typists succeed

Part 4 – Policy: breaks and undo culture (about 5 minutes)

Class and workplace culture can normalize breaks, undo, and “fix it forward” instead of shame. Policies encode whether limits are respected.

Real-life scenario: A club requires continuous 2-hour data entry with no stretch break. Errors rise; blame rises faster.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Draft one policy line that respects human limits.

Part 5 – Advocacy memo (about 5 minutes)

Do this: Write a short memo to a teacher/IT team: one human-limit problem in a school system + one respectful fix.

Keep a respectful tone—facts, impact, request.

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.
