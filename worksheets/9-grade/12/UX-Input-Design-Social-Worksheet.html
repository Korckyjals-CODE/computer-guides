Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Input UX has ethical weight: dark patterns, accessibility, and informed consent. Designers choose whether users are respected or tricked.

Do this:

  1. Describe a form that felt confusing or pushy.
  2. Circle an ethical goal: Informed choices · Hide the opt-out path

Part 2 – Dark patterns in input (about 5 minutes)

Dark patterns include sneaky pre-checked boxes, confusing double negatives (“Uncheck to not not share”), and confirmshaming (“No, I hate saving money”).

Real-life scenario: A free app’s signup uses a giant “Share all contacts” button and a tiny gray “Customize” link.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: How does that design steer behavior unfairly?

Part 3 – Accessibility as social duty (about 5 minutes)

Accessible input (labels for screen readers, keyboard reachability, contrast) is a social obligation, not a niche extra.

Real-life scenario: A stylish form uses light-gray placeholder-only labels. Screen reader users hear empty fields; low-vision users struggle.

Critical thinking: Who is harmed when accessibility is skipped to “look clean”?

Do this:

  1. List two accessibility requirements for a fair school form.
  2. Circle better practice: Visible labels + ARIA/name · Placeholder-only text

Part 4 – Consent and minors (about 5 minutes)

School tools collecting data from minors need clearer consent language and less aggressive defaults. Peers should not pressure each other to click “Allow all.”

Real-life scenario: Friends urge “Just smash Allow” on a permissions screen so a game loads faster—camera and mic included.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: What questions should you ask before Allow?
