## Part 2 – A benefit that can overreach (about 5 minutes)

A city can use streetlight sensors to time traffic lights so buses and pedestrians wait less. The same hardware can sometimes estimate how many phones pass, how long people linger, or which corner is busy at night. The first use schedules a signal. The second use describes people’s movements. People walking to school did not sit in the meeting where that choice was made.

**Real-life scenario:** The city proposes sensors on streetlights along the route to your school. The stated goal is shorter waits at two crossings. The vendor’s brochure also offers “crowd paths” and “dwell time” for shops.

**Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing:** Which use is justified by the crossing problem? Which use should be limited or refused? Who is missing from the decision?

## Part 3 – Who the data belongs to (about 5 minutes)

Three groups often claim IoT data. The **city** says it paid for safer streets. The **vendor** says its software created the reports and its contract allows reuse. The **people counted** say the data describes them in a place they have to use. A contract can say one thing. A fair policy still has to say how long data is kept, whether it can be sold, and how someone can ask what was collected about a place they use every day.

**Critical thinking:** If the vendor may sell “anonymized” path data to advertisers, does removing names finish the question? Write what can still be learned about a neighborhood, and one contract limit you would require.

## Part 4 – Always on, often replaced (about 5 minutes)

IoT hardware uses energy even when the benefit is a small report. Cheap sensors are sometimes replaced on a short cycle because a newer model is easier to manage than an old one. That creates **e-waste** and a stream of devices that must be set up, secured, and disposed of. A system that is slightly less fancy and lasts longer can be the more responsible design.

**Real-life scenario:** Option A replaces streetlight sensors every three years and stores detailed movement data. Option B uses fewer sensors, keeps them for seven years, and stores only hourly crossing totals.
