IoT: Social Worksheet

Grade 9 · Worksheet · About 25 minutes
Work with pen and paper. Use color pencils if you like. You do not need a computer or the internet.

How to use this worksheet
  • Do the parts in order from top to bottom.
  • Each part has a time so you know how long to spend.
  • Read every instruction before you write. You can do this on your own.

Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Connected sensors in public places affect people who never bought a device and never opened an account. In this worksheet you will set limits on a useful city system, ask who owns the data, weigh the cost of devices that are always on and often replaced, and write a recommendation that keeps a benefit without pretending everyone agreed.

Do this:

  1. Name one public place where a sensor system could help (a street, a bus stop, a school entrance, a park).
  2. Write one sentence about who uses that place but would not be the buyer of the system.

Part 2 – A benefit that can overreach (about 5 minutes)

A city can use streetlight sensors to time traffic lights so buses and pedestrians wait less. The same hardware can sometimes estimate how many phones pass, how long people linger, or which corner is busy at night. The first use schedules a signal. The second use describes people’s movements. People walking to school did not sit in the meeting where that choice was made.

Real-life scenario: The city proposes sensors on streetlights along the route to your school. The stated goal is shorter waits at two crossings. The vendor’s brochure also offers “crowd paths” and “dwell time” for shops.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Which use is justified by the crossing problem? Which use should be limited or refused? Who is missing from the decision?

Do this: Sort the features. Write Keep, Limit, or Refuse.

Feature Keep, limit, or refuse? Reason
Count vehicles so a light can change
Estimate how long people stand outside a particular shop
Keep a record that follows the same phone along the whole walking route
Publish a monthly total of pedestrians at the crossing

Part 3 – Who the data belongs to (about 5 minutes)

Three groups often claim IoT data. The city says it paid for safer streets. The vendor says its software created the reports and its contract allows reuse. The people counted say the data describes them in a place they have to use. A contract can say one thing. A fair policy still has to say how long data is kept, whether it can be sold, and how someone can ask what was collected about a place they use every day.

Critical thinking: If the vendor may sell “anonymized” path data to advertisers, does removing names finish the question? Write what can still be learned about a neighborhood, and one contract limit you would require.

Do this: Decide who should control each decision. Write City, Vendor, People who use the street, or More than one.

Decision Who should control it? Why
How long raw sensor data is stored
Whether path data can be sold
Whether a crossing total is published each month
Turning the extra “dwell time” feature off

Part 4 – Always on, often replaced (about 5 minutes)

IoT hardware uses energy even when the benefit is a small report. Cheap sensors are sometimes replaced on a short cycle because a newer model is easier to manage than an old one. That creates e-waste and a stream of devices that must be set up, secured, and disposed of. A system that is slightly less fancy and lasts longer can be the more responsible design.

Real-life scenario: Option A replaces streetlight sensors every three years and stores detailed movement data. Option B uses fewer sensors, keeps them for seven years, and stores only hourly crossing totals.

Do this: Compare the options, then choose.

Question Option A Option B
Detail available to the city
Energy and replacement waste
Risk of describing individuals
Your choice (A or B) and one sentence why

A useful recommendation does not say only “yes” or “no.” It names the benefit to keep, the limit to require, and the question that is still open.

Part 6 – Check what you learned (about 2 minutes)

Checklist. Put a ✓ in the box when it is true for you.

  • □ I can separate a public benefit of IoT from a use that describes people’s movements too closely.
  • □ I can say who claims ownership of sensor data and which claim I would limit by contract.
  • □ I can explain why “anonymized” path data can still describe a neighborhood.
  • □ I can compare a data-heavy, short-lived system with a simpler one that lasts longer.
  • □ I can write a recommendation that includes a benefit, a limit, and an open question, and I can mark where a sensor belongs on a route.

When all boxes are checked, you have reviewed the main ideas from this worksheet.
