## Part 2 – Trust and everyday dependence (about 5 minutes)

People plan their day around systems they **trust** to work: alarms, transit apps, class portals, payment cards. Repeated small failures erode trust even if “the tech usually works.” Reliability is social as well as technical—users stop relying on tools they cannot count on.

**Real-life scenario:** The lunch payment system fails twice a week. Some students carry cash; others cannot. Friends cover meals. Staff scramble with paper lists. Students joke that “the app never works,” and many stop loading money online.

**Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing:** How does unreliable tech change people’s behavior even on days the system works?

## Part 3 – Unequal impact when systems fail (about 5 minutes)

Outages do not hit everyone the same way. Students with limited home internet, one shared device, or tight after-school jobs may have fewer backup options. Fair responses consider who is most affected—not only whether “the network is back.”

**Real-life scenario:** Online registration for elective clubs opens at 7:00 a.m. The site crashes for 40 minutes. Students with strong home Wi-Fi retry and get spots. Others share a phone on slow data and miss out.

## Part 4 – Habits, honesty, and shared responsibility (about 5 minutes)

Reliability improves when people report problems early, follow care rules (don’t overload carts, don’t unplug charging strips), and share honest status updates instead of silence or rumor. Hiding outages to “avoid panic” often creates more panic.

**Real-life scenario:** Wi-Fi drops during a timed quiz. No announcement for an hour. Rumors say grades were wiped. Actually only the connection failed; work was saved. A short, honest message would have calmed the room.
