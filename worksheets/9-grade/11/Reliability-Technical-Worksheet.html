Part 1 – What you will learn (about 3 minutes)

What you will learn: Reliability means a system does what users need, when they need it, for as long as expected. You will explore uptime, redundancy (backup parts or paths), and common failure modes—ways systems can stop working—so you can reason about dependable digital systems.

Do this:

  1. Name one system you rely on every school day (Wi-Fi, bus schedule app, classroom projector, shared drive).
  2. In one short phrase, what goes wrong when that system is unreliable?

Part 2 – Uptime and availability (about 5 minutes)

Uptime is the time a system is working and reachable. Downtime is when it is not. People often talk about availability as a percentage (e.g. “up 99% of the month”), but what matters most is whether the system is available during the hours you actually need it.

Real-life scenario: The school portal was online all weekend but offline Monday 8:00–9:30 a.m. while students submit homework. Weekend uptime looked fine in a report—yet the outage hit the most important window.

Discuss with a partner or reflect in writing: Why can high average uptime still feel unreliable to users?

Part 3 – Redundancy: backup paths and spare parts (about 5 minutes)

Redundancy means having an extra path or spare so one failure does not stop everything. Examples: a second Wi-Fi access point, a spare charger, dual internet links, or saving a file both on a laptop and in a cloud folder. Redundancy costs more (money, space, setup) but raises reliability.

Real-life scenario: Lab A has one network switch. When it fails, every computer loses the internet. Lab B has two switches; half the room stays online if one fails.

Critical thinking: Which lab is designed for higher reliability, and what trade-off does Lab B accept?

Part 4 – Failure modes (about 5 minutes)

A failure mode is a specific way something can break or stop working: power loss, overheating, cable unplugged, software crash, full storage, forgotten password, or slow overload. Listing likely failure modes helps you plan prevention (care, updates, backups) instead of being surprised.

Real-life scenario: A classroom Chromebook cart worked for months. One morning half the devices are dead: the cart’s charging strip was unplugged overnight (power failure mode). Another day a device won’t open files because storage is full (capacity failure mode).

Do this:

  1. List three failure modes for a shared classroom laptop (be specific).
  2. For one failure mode, write one simple prevention step.

Part 5 – Reliability sketch (about 5 minutes)

Do this:

  1. Sketch a simple reliable setup for one school service (Wi-Fi, file storage, or printing). Show the main path and at least one redundant/backup idea. Label a failure mode you are guarding against.
  1. Explain in one or two sentences how your sketch improves uptime or recovery compared with a single-path design.
